## 2025-03-26: Exploratory Analysis Milestone
### Exploratory
The original crash dataset from the Iowa Department of Transportation, along with a dataset on vehicles involved in the collisions, was joined by the "case id" after cleaning both datasets individually. A key issue I found after joining the datasets is that there are duplicate entries for accidents involving multiple vehicles. This raises some questions, such as: Should all drivers be included in analyses, or should I focus on identifying the 'primary driver' for each incident? Another challenge was dealing with outliers. For example, the Blood Alcohol Content (BAC) variable ranged from 0 to 25. Values over 0.5 are highly unrealistic, so I removed values exceeding 0.5. Additionally, driver age had an unusually wide range, from 0 to 200 years old, with strange values like 6, 7, 8, and 102, 153, 200. I removed ages under 10 and over 102, but I may decide to remove ages between 10 and 16 as well. Despite these issues, some interesting trends emerged, such as the age of drivers peaking at around 18, declining steadily until 50, with a slight increase at age 50 before continuing to drop, and a small peak near age 100. For exploratory analysis, I created a map of Iowa's counties, colored by the total number of crashes in each, using a log scale to account for the wide differences in crash frequency. In my dashboard, I would like to expand this by allowing users to filter crashes by year. I also created a histogram of driver ages and a bar chart showing the frequency of number of accidents per weekday, both of which I would like to include in my dashboard as well.

## 2025-03-16: Finalize Data Models Milestone
### Finalize Data Model
I am using two different models for my project: a random forest and a LASSO regression. The first model I explored was a random forest. I initially intended to use “Fatal” as the response variable, which indicates whether a crash was fatal or not, but I found that only 0.57% of crashes in the dataset were fatal. Because of this extreme imbalance, I decided to use “Crash.Severity” instead. This categorical variable has five levels: Property Damage Only, Possible/Unknown, Minor Injury, Major Injury, and Fatal. To run the random forest, I had to thoroughly clean the dataset by addressing null values —this included removing rows with NAs in some columns and recoding NAs in others. The explanatory variables I included in the model are: Month.of.Crash, Day.of.Week, Hour, County.Name, First.Harmful.Event, Manner.of.Crash.Collision, Major.Cause, Drug.or.Alcohol, Environmental.Conditions, Light.Conditions, Surface.Conditions, Weather.Conditions, Work.Zone, Number.of.Injuries, Number.of.Major.Injuries, Number.of.Minor.Injuries, Number.of.Possible.Injuries, Number.of.Unknown.Injuries, Amount.of.Property.Damage, Number.of.Vehicles.Involved, Total.Number.of.Occupants, Year, DRIVERAGE, DRIVERGEN, DL_STATE, CHARGED, ALCRESULT, DRUGRESULT, MAKE, MODEL, and SPEEDLIMIT. The research question this model aims to answer is: “Which factors are the strongest predictors of the severity of a car crash?” According to the variable importance plot from the random forest, the most important predictors include whether the driver was charged, the number of major and minor injuries, total injuries, drug test results, the driver’s age, the amount of property damage, and the make of the car.

The second model I am using is a LASSO regression, where the response variable is the amount of property damage in dollars, which is a continuous numeric variable. I chose LASSO because I have 31 predictors, and I do not believe all of them are equally informative. LASSO performs variable selection by shrinking some coefficients to zero, helping to simplify the model and improve interpretability. To determine the optimal value of lambda, I used 10-fold cross-validation. This model is designed to answer the research question: “Is it possible to predict the amount of property damage for a car crash?” This model will be interactive in my dashboard, in which the user can input custom values for each explanatory variable and predict the amount of property damage for their hypothetical crash.

The biggest obstacle I’ve encountered while modeling has been the size of my dataset, which contains over 500,000 rows. Running the full random forest model took over 10 minutes, and my initial attempt at fitting the LASSO model failed due to memory limitations. To manage this, I sampled a subset of the data for both models. 

My data is not time-series or spatial. The datasets that are used for model building are fully cleaned. The assumptions made about my data for both models are that all of the car crashes are independent of each other, and additional assumptions made about my data for lasso is that there is a linear relationship between my predictors and my response variable, and none of my predictors are perfectly correlated with each other.
